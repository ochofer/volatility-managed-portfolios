Volatility-managed portfolios on French's factors: the replication, the real-time critique, and the 136 months after the sample

Carlo Hofer. November 2026. The note of the project Volatility-managed portfolios. Code, notebooks and the tables every number comes from: github.com/ochofer/volatility-managed-portfolios, results/202609.

Moreira and Muir (2017) scale a factor's position each month by the inverse of its realised variance in the month before, and report alphas, the intercepts of the regression of the scaled factor on the original, that are positive and significant for the market, momentum and profitability among the six factors studied here. Cederburg, O'Doherty, Wang and Yan (2020) show that an investor who had to estimate in real time how to combine the scaled and the original factor would have gained little. This note replicates both papers and Barroso and Santa-Clara's (2015) scaled momentum on Kenneth French's six factors, tests every difference between two Sharpe ratios (the mean excess return over its standard deviation, annualised) with Ledoit and Wolf's (2008) bootstrap, and asks what the signal did in the 136 months after Moreira and Muir's sample, May 2015 to August 2026. Six expectations were written before any code judged them, and four are met. The replications hold for 6 of 6 factors in Moreira and Muir's Table I and in 4 of 4 comparisons for scaled momentum, and in 22 of 24 comparisons for the real-time critique, an expectation not met because French has revised the profitability factor's history since the paper. The expectation that the Sharpe ratio test and the alpha's t-statistic agree is not met either, 2 of 6 factors where 5 were expected, because the two test different things: the volatility-managed Sharpe ratio is above the original's beyond its interval for momentum alone, +0.51, annualised, with an interval of 0.07 to 0.96. After the sample, no factor's real-time combination has a Sharpe ratio above the original's by more than its interval allows, the median difference +0.007, annualised, against a median half-width of 0.213, so these months cannot tell the differences from zero. The market's alpha net of 10 basis points per unit of turnover is 4.03 per cent a year (t 2.31), and 3.13 (t 1.81) net of 20.

Terms used in this note, each with the one meaning it carries throughout.

factor
the monthly return of a long-short portfolio that captures one source of common movement in stock returns: the market (Mkt-RF), size (SMB), value (HML), momentum (Mom), profitability (RMW) and investment (CMA) of Fama and French (2015) and Carhart (1997), as Kenneth French publishes them.
excess return
a return minus the one-month Treasury bill rate; every factor is one.
realised variance
the variance of a factor computed from its daily returns within one month, each paper with its own definition.
the signal
last month's realised variance of a factor, and nothing else.
volatility-managed factor
Moreira and Muir's construction, the factor's return times c over last month's realised variance; the word managed describes the construction and says nothing about its performance.
c
the constant that gives the volatility-managed factor the original's standard deviation over the whole sample, so it is known only at the sample's end.
position
the multiple of the factor held in a month, c over last month's realised variance for the volatility-managed factor. The papers call a position above 1 leverage.
real-time combination (S1)
Cederburg et al.'s construction: the mean-variance weights on the volatility-managed and the original factor, with a risk aversion of 5, estimated each month from the months before it, from 120 months on, the total position capped at 5.
real-time original (S2)
the original factor alone at its real-time mean-variance weight, S1's benchmark.
ex post optimal combination (S3)
the mix of the two with the weights that were best over the whole sample, which no investor could have known.
benchmark
the strategy a construction is compared with.
scaled momentum
Barroso and Santa-Clara's construction, WML times 12 per cent a year over its realised volatility of the previous 126 trading days.
WML
winners minus losers, the top momentum decile's return minus the bottom decile's.
alpha
the intercept of the regression of the volatility-managed factor on its original, in per cent a year; it measures what the volatility-managed factor adds to a holding of the original.
beta
the slope of that regression.
standard error
the standard deviation of an estimate across the samples it could have come from; White's allows the residual's variance to change, and Newey and West's at 12 lags also allows a month's residual to be correlated with the 12 before it.
Sharpe ratio
the mean excess return over its standard deviation, annualised by the square root of 12.
appraisal ratio
the alpha over the residual's standard deviation, annualised.
Jobson-Korkie test
a test that two return series over the same months have the same Sharpe ratio, assuming normal and independent months, with Memmel's (2003) correction.
interval
the 95 per cent interval of Ledoit and Wolf's test for the true Sharpe ratio difference; the test rejects when it excludes zero.
half-width
the distance from a difference to either end of its interval.
delta method
the standard error of a function of means, from the function's slopes and the means' covariance.
fat tails
extreme months that occur more often than a normal distribution allows.
degrees of freedom
the parameter of a Student-t distribution that sets how fat its tails are; the fewer, the fatter.
Student-t
a bell-shaped distribution with fatter tails than the normal one, here with five degrees of freedom.
kurtosis
excess kurtosis, a measure of how often extreme values occur, 0 for the normal distribution.
p-value
the probability of a difference at least as far from zero as the one computed, if the two true Sharpe ratios were equal.
size
the share of samples in which a test rejects when the null is true.
turnover
the absolute change in the position from the month before, the first month carrying none.
basis point
a hundredth of a percentage point.
break-even cost
the cost per unit of turnover at which the alpha falls to zero.
maximum drawdown
the largest fall from a peak of the compounded return.
band
the distance from a published number within which a computed one counts as matching, fixed before the code.
verdict
whether an expectation is met; a count reported in no verdict is shown without one.
vintage
the version of French's files, named by the CRSP release they were built from.

1. Introduction

A volatility-managed portfolio holds a factor in inverse proportion to the variance the factor showed in the month before, less after a volatile month and more after a calm one. Moreira and Muir (2017) applied the rule to the market and to the standard equity factors from 1926 to April 2015 and found alphas against the unscaled factors that are positive and significant for the market, momentum and profitability among the six factors studied here, the market's 4.86 per cent a year with a standard error of 1.56. The rule needs one number from the past, last month's realised variance, and one constant, c, which sets the position's average size and is chosen on the whole sample. The question this note asks is how much of that result an investor could have used: whether it reproduces on French's data as they stand today, whether it survives the combination of the managed and the original factor being estimated in real time, and what the signal did in the months after the paper's sample ended.

The existing evidence answers the second part and leaves the third open. Cederburg, O'Doherty, Wang and Yan (2020) argue that an alpha against the original factor is the gain of an investor who already holds the original and adds the managed factor in the amount the regression implies, an amount known only at the sample's end, like c. When they estimate that amount each month from the months before it, the combination's Sharpe ratio on the six factors of this note falls below the original's for four of them and differs significantly only for momentum, where it is higher. Their test of a Sharpe ratio difference is Jobson and Korkie's, which assumes normal and independent months, and Moreira and Muir report alphas, so neither paper gives a test of the Sharpe ratio difference that allows for fat tails and for volatile months that follow each other. Neither can say what happened after its sample: Moreira and Muir's ends in April 2015 and Cederburg et al.'s in December 2016.

This note replicates both papers and Barroso and Santa-Clara's (2015) scaled momentum, the third paper's version of the same scaling, on French's six factors, each paper on its own sample and with its own definition of the realised variance, and judges each replication against expectations written before the code ran. It then tests every Sharpe ratio difference with Ledoit and Wolf's (2008) studentised block bootstrap, checked first on simulated pairs whose Sharpe ratios are equal by construction, and carries both constructions forward unchanged to the 136 months from May 2015 to August 2026, with c fixed on the months to April 2015. A cost of 10 basis points per unit of turnover, with 20 as the one sensitivity, prices the trades the signal needs. The design estimates no premium and recommends no position: every return reported is a simulation on French's history, with its standard error or interval beside it, and every number comes from a table committed with the code.

French's factors are an informative setting for these questions for two reasons. Both papers built their results on them and French keeps them current, so the replication and the months after the sample run on one source, and French's revisions of history become visible: a factor whose statistics differ from a paper's on the same months has changed since the paper's download, and an archived copy of the file can show by how much. At the same time, the months after the sample hold the highest realised variance of the market in French's history, March 2020, so the signal was tested in a month in which it cut the position to 0.013 for April, when the market returned 13.6 per cent, and the 136 months are few enough that every difference they give has to be read against its interval.

Empirically, the replications hold where French's data have not changed, and the alpha and the Sharpe ratio separate. Moreira and Muir's Table I reproduces for 6 of 6 factors and scaled momentum in 4 of 4 comparisons; Cederburg et al.'s results reproduce in 22 of 24 comparisons, the two misses on RMW, whose mean French's current file puts at 3.12 per cent a year on their months against their 2.92. On Moreira and Muir's sample the volatility-managed Sharpe ratio is above the original's beyond its interval for momentum alone, +0.51, annualised, with an interval of 0.07 to 0.96, while the alpha's t-statistic is above two for the market, momentum and RMW, and an identity shows why the two tests differ. After the sample no factor's real-time combination has a Sharpe ratio above the real-time original's by more than its interval allows, the median difference +0.007, annualised, against a median half-width of 0.213. These 136 months therefore cannot tell the differences from zero, and they rule out neither a positive nor a negative difference of the size estimated on Moreira and Muir's sample.

2. Design

Data. Kenneth French's data library, the vintage built from the 202609 CRSP database: the three-factor and five-factor files, the momentum factor and the ten portfolios sorted on prior returns, each monthly and daily, downloaded at run time and never committed. The market, SMB and HML start in July 1926, momentum and WML in January 1927 (their daily files on 3 November 1926), RMW and CMA in July 1963. The sample ends in August 2026 whatever the vintage. Each paper's results are compared on its own months: Moreira and Muir's to April 2015, the end their published counts of months imply (1,065 for the market, 621 for RMW and CMA); Cederburg et al.'s to December 2016; Barroso and Santa-Clara's from May 1927 to December 2011. The months after the sample are May 2015 to August 2026.

The signal and the constructions. Each construction uses its paper's own realised variance: Moreira and Muir's sum of squared deviations of the daily returns from the month's mean (their equation 2), Cederburg et al.'s 22 over the month's trading days times the sum of squared daily returns (their equation 4), and Barroso and Santa-Clara's 21 times the mean of the squared daily returns over the previous 126 trading days (their equation 5). The volatility-managed factor holds c over last month's realised variance; c makes its standard deviation over the sample equal to the original's. The real-time combination S1 holds, each month, the mean-variance weights of an investor with a risk aversion of 5 on the volatility-managed and the original factor, estimated from all the months before it once 120 are available, its total position capped at 5; S2 holds the original alone at its real-time weight, and S3 the combination with the weights that were best over the whole sample. Scaled momentum holds WML at 12 per cent a year over its realised volatility of the previous 126 trading days.

The regression and its standard errors. Each volatility-managed factor is regressed on its original, monthly, by least squares. The alpha is the intercept in per cent a year. Moreira and Muir's Table I is judged with White's standard errors, the paper's own; Newey and West's at 12 lags are reported beside them and used for the alpha in sections 3.3 and 3.5. Cederburg et al.'s Sharpe ratio differences are reproduced with their Jobson and Korkie test.

The test of a Sharpe ratio difference. Ledoit and Wolf (2008) write the difference between two Sharpe ratios as a function of four means, those of each series and of its square, and take its standard error by the delta method from the long-run covariance of the four. On the sample the covariance comes from the prewhitened Parzen kernel with Andrews' (1991) bandwidth; the paper proposes the QS kernel and reports the Parzen kernel's performance as very similar. The interval comes from a circular block bootstrap: 2,000 samples of blocks of 12 consecutive months, each sample's difference studentised by its own standard error from its blocks, and the 95th percentile of the absolute studentised differences, z*, replacing the normal table's 1.96. The test rejects when the interval excludes zero. The paper calibrates the block length by its Algorithm 3.1; here it is fixed at 12 months before the run. Before the test touches a factor it is run on 1,000 simulated pairs of 1,065 months with the market's monthly mean and standard deviation, a correlation of 0.601, Student-t shocks with five degrees of freedom and equal Sharpe ratios by construction, to measure its size.

Costs and positions. A month's turnover is the absolute change in the position from the month before, the first month carrying none. The cost, 10 basis points per unit of turnover and 20 as the sensitivity, is subtracted from the volatility-managed return, and the alpha is estimated again on the net return. The factor's own rebalancing is the same for the managed and the original factor and is left out. The break-even cost is the gross alpha over 12 times the average turnover. The position's percentiles are compared with Moreira and Muir's Table V and Cederburg et al.'s Table 1, Panel D.

The expectations. Six expectations were written into the code's constants on 8 and 10 November 2026, each with its reason, before any run that judged them: E1 to E3 on the three replications, E4 on the test's size and on its agreement with the alpha, E5 on the months after the sample, E6 on the costs and the positions. Each is a strict pass or fail, and no level moved after a run. Table 9 states each with what the run found.

3.3 The alpha and the Sharpe ratio difference

The test of a Sharpe ratio difference passes its size check: on the 1,000 simulated pairs it rejects 37 at the 5 per cent level, 3.7 per cent, inside the range of 3.5 to 6.5 fixed before the run as two standard errors of a share of 0.05. Table 5 reports it on Moreira and Muir's sample beside the alpha. The volatility-managed Sharpe ratio is above the original's beyond its interval for momentum alone, by 0.51, annualised, with an interval of 0.07 to 0.96 and a p-value of 0.03. For the market the difference is +0.10 with an interval of −0.11 to 0.31 and a p-value of 0.33, beside an alpha with a Newey-West t-statistic of 2.80; for RMW it is +0.19 with an interval of −0.11 to 0.49, beside a t-statistic of 2.81. E4 expected the two tests to agree in sign and significance for at least five factors, and they agree for two, SMB and momentum, so E4 is not met; by category, in no verdict, they agree for four. The signs of HML's and CMA's alphas, positive, and of their Sharpe ratio differences, negative, were known from the replication before any bootstrap ran, and they already limited the agreement to four of six.

An identity shows why the two tests differ. With the volatility-managed factor scaled to the original's standard deviation σ, the regression's slope equals the correlation ρ between the two, and the volatility-managed factor's mean is α + ρμ, so its Sharpe ratio minus the original's equals α/σ minus (1 − ρ) times the original's Sharpe ratio. Table 5 computes both sides, equal to four decimals for every factor. The alpha therefore measures what the volatility-managed factor adds to a holding of the original, and the difference measures the volatility-managed factor held in the original's place, which gives up the share 1 − ρ of the original's Sharpe ratio. With correlations of 0.45 to 0.67, the alpha per unit of risk has to exceed a third to a half of the original's Sharpe ratio before the difference turns positive: for the market α/σ is 0.264 and (1 − ρ) times the Sharpe ratio 0.167, so the difference is +0.097; for HML they are 0.141 and 0.182, so a positive alpha of 1.73 per cent a year comes with a difference of −0.041. Cederburg et al.'s Table 1 shows the same separation for the market, a difference with a p-value of 0.30 beside Moreira and Muir's t-statistic of 3.1. The sign of a difference on this sample says nothing about its sign on another.

The volatility-managed factor with c fixed on the months to April 2015 gives the same counts, in no verdict: no interval above zero, and a median difference of −0.167 within a median half-width of 0.672. Its Sharpe ratio is below the original's for four factors and above it for momentum and RMW. For the market it is 0.554 against the original's 0.784, a difference of −0.231 with an interval of −1.120 to 0.659. The three months with the largest gaps between the two returns are October 2018, April 2020 and January 2018. In October 2018 the position was 3.56, after a calm September, and the market fell 7.65 per cent while the volatility-managed market fell 27.3 per cent. In April 2020 the position was 0.013, after March 2020, the highest realised variance of the market in French's history, and the market rose 13.6 per cent while the volatility-managed market rose 0.18 per cent. In January 2018 the position was 3.38, and the market rose 5.58 per cent while the volatility-managed market rose 18.9 per cent.

The intervals are wide because the months are 136. A standard error falls with the square root of the number of months, so 136 months in place of 1,065 multiply it by 2.80, and the volatility-managed comparisons' half-widths are 0.53 to 0.89, annualised, here, against 0.21 to 0.44 on Moreira and Muir's sample. These 136 months therefore cannot tell the differences from zero: an interval of −1.12 to 0.66 for the market is consistent with a negative difference and with a positive one of the size estimated on Moreira and Muir's sample, and the sign of each difference says nothing about another sample.

4. Discussion

The natural comparison is with the two papers' own conclusions. On Moreira and Muir's sample the alphas reproduce for every factor, and the market's survives 10 basis points per unit of turnover at 2.31 standard errors, as the paper reports. Cederburg et al.'s conclusion reproduces as well: an investor who estimated the combination in real time had a Sharpe ratio below the real-time original's for four of six factors, by 0.04 to 0.07, above it for momentum by 0.41, the one significant difference, and above it for RMW by 0.18. Ledoit and Wolf's test adds the reading that separates the two papers: held in the original's place, the volatility-managed factor has a higher Sharpe ratio beyond its interval for momentum alone, so an alpha of more than two standard errors is a statement about adding the managed factor to the original, at weights fixed on the whole sample, and the evidence for holding it alone is confined to momentum on this sample.

What the months after the sample imply is therefore narrow. Neither construction shows a Sharpe ratio difference beyond its interval on any factor, and the intervals are wide enough to hold the paper-sample estimates: the market's volatility-managed difference on Moreira and Muir's sample, +0.097, lies inside its after-sample interval of −1.120 to 0.659, as does momentum's, +0.514, inside −0.449 to 0.803. These months are consistent with the signal doing after 2015 what it did before, and with it doing less; they cannot tell the two apart.

At the same time, the design has limits, each with its cause. First, the costs are charged on the change in the position of a factor, and the factors are long-short portfolios whose own trading in the stocks that make them up is left out because it is the same for the managed and the original factor; a book that holds the stocks pays more, and the break-even costs bound what it could pay. Second, French revises history, so a rerun on a later vintage need not reproduce every digit, as RMW shows; every table names its vintage, and the archived copy shows the size of one revision. Third, the block length of 12 months and the Parzen kernel were fixed before the run, where Ledoit and Wolf calibrate the block length from the data and propose the QS kernel. Fourth, c for the months after the sample is fixed on the months to April 2015, the paper's own constant, so the volatility-managed comparison after the sample asks what the paper's construction did next, and the real-time combination asks what an investor estimating it would have done. Finally, the position is never capped for the volatility-managed factor, as in the paper, so its largest positions, up to 16.8, are positions no mandate would allow.

What would settle the question for the months after the sample is more of them. The half-width falls with the square root of the months, so halving the market's S1 minus S2 half-width of 0.639 would require four times the 136 months, about 45 years.

Against this evidence the note supports a statement about the signal on French's six factors over these samples. Scaled by last month's realised variance, the market, momentum and RMW earn alphas against their originals of more than two Newey-West standard errors on Moreira and Muir's sample, the market's net of 10 basis points at 2.31 standard errors and net of 20 at 1.81; held in the original's place, the volatility-managed factor has a higher Sharpe ratio beyond its interval for momentum alone; estimated in real time, the combination's Sharpe ratio is below the original's for four of six factors; and the 136 months since the sample cannot tell any of these differences from zero.

# Volatility-managed portfolios: a replication, the real-time critique, and the months after the sample

I replicate Moreira and Muir (2017), "Volatility-Managed Portfolios" (Journal of Finance 72(4), 1611-1644), on Ken French's data: a factor's position each month is scaled by the inverse of the factor's realised variance in the month before. I then replicate the real-time critique of Cederburg, O'Doherty, Wang and Yan (2020), "On the performance of volatility-managed portfolios" (Journal of Financial Economics 138(1), 95-117), in which the weights an investor needs are estimated from the data available at the time, and Barroso and Santa-Clara's (2015) scaled momentum as the one robustness check. Sharpe ratio differences are tested with Ledoit and Wolf's (2008) bootstrap. The study's own question is what the signal did in the 136 months after Moreira and Muir's sample, May 2015 to August 2026. Of the six expectations written before any code judged them, four are met and two are not: the real-time critique reproduces in 22 of its 24 comparisons, the two misses on the one factor whose history French has revised since the paper (E2), and the Sharpe ratio test agrees with the t-statistic on the alpha, the intercept of the regression of the volatility-managed factor on its original, for 2 of 6 factors where 5 were expected, because the two test different things (E4). In the months after the sample no factor's real-time Sharpe ratio difference lies outside its interval, and the intervals are wide: these 136 months cannot tell the differences from zero.

## Findings

On Moreira and Muir's sample, each factor's first month to April 2015 (1,065 months for the market, 621 for profitability and investment), the regression of each volatility-managed factor on its original reproduces their Table I for 6 of 6 factors (E1). The alpha, in per cent a year, has the published sign and 5 per cent significance for every factor and lies within one published standard error (the standard deviation of the estimate across the samples it could have come from) of the published alpha, and the appraisal ratio, the alpha over the residual's standard deviation, lies within 0.05 of the published one. The market's alpha is 4.93 per cent a year with a White standard error of 1.57, against the paper's 4.86 and 1.56. Barroso and Santa-Clara's scaled momentum reproduces in 4 of 4 comparisons on May 1927 to December 2011 (E3): the Sharpe ratio, the mean excess return (the return over the one-month Treasury bill rate) over its standard deviation, annualised, is 0.54 for momentum and 1.00 scaled, against 0.53 and 0.97, and the worst fall from a peak is 95.6 and 43.8 per cent, against 96.7 and 45.2, all inside the bands of 0.05 and five points fixed before the code.

The real-time critique reproduces in 22 of Cederburg, O'Doherty, Wang and Yan's 24 comparisons on their sample to December 2016, and E2 is not met. The two misses are both RMW's: its volatility-managed Sharpe ratio is 0.566 against Table 1's 0.51, and the real-time combination's 0.510 against Table 5's 0.44, 0.006 and 0.020 outside the band. The cause is French's revision of RMW since the paper's download: on the same 641 months its mean is now 3.12 per cent a year against Table 1's 2.92, with the same standard deviation (7.70 against 7.71). Two diagnostics, computed after the verdict (the expectation's pass or fail) and carrying none, point the same way. With RMW's monthly returns moved by one constant to Table 1's mean, the two gaps close by 60 and 61 per cent (the volatility-managed Sharpe ratio's from 0.056 to 0.022, the combination's from 0.070 to 0.027), and the Internet Archive's copy of French's five-factor file of 9 July 2017 gives RMW a mean of 2.89 and a standard deviation of 7.74 on those months. The other comparisons hold: the real-time combination minus the real-time original (S2) has the published sign for 6 of 6 factors, momentum's +0.41 is the one difference significant at 5 per cent by Jobson and Korkie's test, as in Table 5, and the real-time combination falls short of the ex post optimal combination (S3) by 0.08 to 0.12, against the published 0.07 to 0.14.

The study tests a difference between two Sharpe ratios with Ledoit and Wolf's (2008) studentised circular block bootstrap, which resamples the months in blocks of 12 to see how much the difference would vary on another sample of the same length. On 1,000 simulated pairs of 1,065 months whose Sharpe ratios are equal by construction it rejects 3.7 per cent at the 5 per cent level, inside the range of 3.5 to 6.5 fixed before the run. On Moreira and Muir's sample it finds the volatility-managed Sharpe ratio above the original's for momentum alone, by 0.51, annualised, with a 95 per cent interval of 0.07 to 0.96. For the market the difference is 0.10, annualised, with an interval of -0.11 to 0.31 (p-value 0.33, the probability of a difference this far from zero if the two true Sharpe ratios were equal), beside an alpha whose Newey-West t-statistic is 2.80. E4 expected the bootstrap and the t-statistic on the alpha to agree in sign and in significance for at least five of the six factors. They agree for two, SMB and momentum, so E4 is not met, and by category, in no verdict, they agree for four.

The expectation rested on a reason that does not hold, since the alpha and the Sharpe ratio difference test different things. With the volatility-managed factor scaled to the original's standard deviation σ, the regression's slope equals the correlation ρ between the two, and the Sharpe ratio difference equals α/σ minus (1 - ρ) times the original's Sharpe ratio, both sides equal to four decimals for all six factors. The alpha measures what the volatility-managed factor adds to a holding of the original, and the difference measures it held in the original's place. With correlations of 0.45 to 0.67, the alpha per unit of risk has to exceed a third to a half of the original's Sharpe ratio before the difference turns positive. HML's and CMA's alphas are positive while their differences are negative, so E4's second part could reach at most 4 of 6 whatever the bootstrap gave, and notebook 06 records that this was known on 12 November 2026, before any bootstrap ran. Cederburg et al.'s Table 1 shows the same split for the market: a difference with a p-value of 0.30 beside Moreira and Muir's t-statistic of 3.1.

The study's own question is what the signal did in the 136 months after Moreira and Muir's sample, May 2015 to August 2026. Moreira and Muir's c, the constant that scales each month's position, is fixed on the months to April 2015, and the real-time combination uses only the months before each decision, so no parameter of either construction was set on these months (Cederburg et al.'s own sample covers the first 20 of them). For no factor does the interval for the real-time combination's Sharpe ratio minus the real-time original's lie above zero, and the median difference, +0.007, annualised, lies within the median half-width of the six intervals, 0.213: E5 is met. The six differences run from -0.090 for HML, with an interval of -0.234 to 0.053, to +0.198 for momentum, with an interval of -0.342 to 0.738. The volatility-managed factor against the original, in no verdict, gives the same counts. Its Sharpe ratio is below the original's for four of the six factors, the market's 0.55 against 0.78, a difference of -0.23 with an interval of -1.12 to 0.66, and the median difference, -0.167, lies within the median half-width, 0.672. The intervals are wide because the months are 136: the volatility-managed comparisons' half-widths are 0.53 to 0.89, annualised, on these months, against 0.21 to 0.44 on the paper's sample. These 136 months cannot tell the differences from zero, and the sign of a difference on them says nothing about its sign on another sample.

E6 prices the trades the signal needs and measures the size of the position. The volatility-managed market's position changes by 0.75 of the factor a month on average on Moreira and Muir's sample, against the paper's 0.73, and turnover, that absolute change, is what the cost is charged on. At 10 basis points (hundredths of a per cent) per unit of turnover the market's alpha is 4.03 per cent a year with a Newey-West standard error of 1.74 (t 2.31), against the paper's 3.98, and at 20 basis points it is 3.13 with a standard error of 1.73 (t 1.81). The break-even cost, at which the alpha reaches zero, is 55 basis points per unit of turnover, against the paper's 56. Momentum's alpha is 11.70 per cent a year net of 10 basis points (t 5.82) and 10.56 net of 20 (t 5.37), RMW's 2.06 (t 2.09) and 1.32 (t 1.35), and those of SMB, HML and CMA lie within two standard errors of zero at both costs. The market's position has a median of 0.94 and a 99th percentile of 7.27, against the paper's 0.93 and 6.39, and its largest, 16.8 in March 1964, followed the market's calmest month of the sample, February 1964. On Cederburg et al.'s sample every factor's median position lies within 0.03 of their Table 1, Panel D, and every 99th percentile within 0.48. E6 is met, 17 of 17.

Against this evidence the study supports a statement about the signal on French's six factors over these samples. Scaled by last month's realised variance, the market, momentum and RMW earn alphas against their originals of more than two Newey-West standard errors on Moreira and Muir's sample, the market's net of 10 basis points per unit of turnover at 2.31 standard errors and net of 20 at 1.81, while held in the original's place the volatility-managed factor has a Sharpe ratio above the original's beyond its interval for momentum alone. An investor who estimated the combination's weights in real time would have had a Sharpe ratio below the real-time original's on four of six factors on Cederburg et al.'s sample, by 0.04 to 0.07, and above it for momentum by 0.41, the one significant difference, and for RMW by 0.18. In the 136 months after the sample the intervals cannot tell any factor's difference from zero. The study holds no view on expected returns. It charges costs on the change in the position alone, since the factor's own rebalancing is the same for the managed and the original factor, and it reads French's files of the 202609 vintage, which French revises, as the RMW miss shows. The note states the limits in its last section.

## What is pre-committed

Every sample boundary, construction parameter and expectation is in `src/sq/constants.py`, each with its source and date, written before any code that judges it. The published numbers the replication compares against are in `src/sq/published.py`, each with its page and table; `tests/test_published.py` re-reads them digit by digit from the journal PDFs when the PDFs are present on the machine (they are not in the repository). ## How to run

Each notebook runs from a blank Google Colab runtime: upload it and run all cells. It writes the code it needs into `src/sq/` and downloads French's files at run time; nothing from French's library is committed. The sample ends at August 2026, fixed in the code (`SAMPLE_END`). A run on a later vintage, cut at August 2026, is a confirmation run: it is labelled by its vintage in the provenance record, it is expected to reproduce every verdict line and every printed decimal and not every digit, because French revises history, and it replaces no committed table without a dated decision.

From a checkout: `pip install -r requirements.txt`, then `python3 -m pytest tests`, then open the notebooks. `build_notebooks.py` regenerates every notebook from its prose and the modules in `src/sq/`; the notebooks are build artefacts and are committed without outputs.

## Terms

- **volatility-managed**: Moreira and Muir's construction, the factor's return times a constant divided by last month's realised variance. Managed describes the construction and says nothing about its performance.
- **real-time combination**: Cederburg, O'Doherty, Wang and Yan's construction, a mix of the volatility-managed and the original factor whose weights are estimated each month from the months before it.
- **the signal**: last month's realised variance of a factor, and nothing else.
- **realised variance**: the variance of a factor computed from the daily returns of one month, each paper with its own definition, given in `src/sq/constants.py`.
- **position**: the multiple of the factor held in a month; the papers call a position above 1 leverage.
- **WML**: winners minus losers, the top momentum decile's return minus the bottom decile's, Barroso and Santa-Clara's momentum series.
- **trading day**: a day on which the New York Stock Exchange traded, one row of French's daily files.
- **vintage**: the version of French's files on the download date, named by the CRSP release they were built from.
- **sample end**: August 2026, the last month any notebook uses, whatever the vintage.
- **real-time original (S2)**: the original factor alone, held each month at the mean-variance weight estimated from the months before it, Cederburg et al.'s benchmark for the real-time combination (S1).
- **ex post optimal combination (S3)**: the mix of the volatility-managed and the original factor with the weights that were best over the whole sample, which no investor could have known.
- **alpha**: the intercept of the regression of a volatility-managed factor on its original, in per cent a year, always with its standard error or t-statistic.
- **Sharpe ratio**: the mean excess return over its standard deviation, annualised.
- **appraisal ratio**: the alpha over the standard deviation of the regression's residual, annualised.
- **interval**: the 95 per cent interval of Ledoit and Wolf's test for the true Sharpe ratio difference, and a difference is significant when its interval excludes zero.
- **half-width**: the distance from a difference to either end of its interval.
- **size check**: the test run on simulated pairs whose Sharpe ratios are equal by construction, to measure how often it rejects a true null.
- **turnover**: the absolute change in the position from one month to the next, the first month carrying none.
- **break-even cost**: the cost per unit of turnover at which the alpha falls to zero.
- **maximum drawdown**: the largest fall from a peak of the compounded return.
